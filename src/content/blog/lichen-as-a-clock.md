title: Lichen as a clock
description: Some lichens grow slowly enough, and predictably enough, that they can date the rock they're on.

---

Certain crustose lichens grow at a rate steady enough to be used for dating exposed rock
surfaces — a technique called lichenometry, originally developed for glacial moraines. A lichen
on a boundary stone or a gravestone becomes, in effect, a slow ring gauge: measure the largest
thallus, apply a local growth curve, and you have a rough date for when that surface was first
exposed.

What stuck with me wasn't the technique so much as the timescale. Most of what I write about
in this notebook happens in minutes — the length of time it takes to notice a chiton, or wait
for a moth to land. Lichen operates in millimeters per decade. It's the same [slow attention](/blog/the-slow-eye/)
in a completely different gear, and it made me want to keep a page in [the field notebook](/blog/keeping-a-field-notebook/)
specifically for things I can only check once a year.

I've marked one lichen patch on a wall near the house. Next measurement: same week, next year.
